## Phase 1 — Defender: Opening Statement

**Goal.** Cut p95 read latency on `/products` and `/products/<id>` from ~180ms and insulate reads from Postgres load spikes during the catalog team's bulk imports. Catalog is small (~12k products, ~25MB serialized) and changes rarely (~50 writes/day), which makes it an unusually cache-friendly workload.

**Key decisions and why:**

1. **In-process dict cache, per gunicorn worker.** The latency goal is the point of the exercise: a dict hit is sub-microsecond and removes both the DB round trip and any network hop. The catalog fits in memory trivially; duplicating 25MB across 8 workers (~200MB total) is cheap on our hosts. Operationally it is zero new infrastructure — no Redis to provision, secure, monitor, or fail over.

2. **Lazy population on miss.** Simplest possible code path; no warm-up job, no startup coupling to Postgres availability. First request per key per worker pays the DB cost, everything after is free until TTL expiry.

3. **TTL-only invalidation, 10 minutes.** The PM has explicitly accepted up to 10 minutes of staleness. TTL-only means no invalidation bus, no pub/sub, no cache-coherence protocol — the entire class of "invalidation is one of the two hard problems" bugs is bought off with a product-approved staleness budget.

4. **`all_products` as a single cached key.** The list endpoint is the hot path during imports; caching the assembled list avoids re-serializing 12k rows per request.

5. **Rejected alternatives.** Redis: adds a network hop per read (works against the latency goal) and new infra for a dataset that fits in RAM. CDN: responses carry per-region pricing, so edge caching needs cache-key design we don't want to fund now.

6. **Rollout.** `CATALOG_CACHE` env flag, staging for a week, then production — cheap to revert.

**Decisions I'm least sure of:** (a) the interaction between the 10-minute TTL and 8 independent workers — different workers will hold different vintages of the same product, so two consecutive requests from one user can disagree; (b) `all_products` and per-id entries expiring independently, which can make the list and the detail views inconsistent with each other; (c) behavior at TTL expiry under concurrency — a stampede of workers re-reading Postgres at once, which is the very moment (bulk import) we are trying to protect against.

### Defender's answers

Facts where the design or context fixes them; explicitly-labeled working assumptions where it does not (no live stakeholder to ask).

1. **Per-region pricing vs. cache (Adversary Q1, Judge Q1).** Intent: the cache stores the region-neutral product record as serialized from Postgres; per-region pricing is applied after the cache read, at response-assembly time, from the pricing component the endpoints already use. So the cache key is `product_id` only and the 25MB/worker estimate stands. Honest admission: the doc's phrase `serialized_product` does not pin this down, and if an implementer cached the rendered response instead, the cache would silently serve region A's prices to region B or need region-suffixed keys (memory × number of regions). This must be made explicit in the design.

2. **Traffic numbers (Adversary Q2, Advocate Q1, Judge Q3).** Working assumptions, order-of-magnitude: combined read traffic ~200–400 RPS, roughly 60% `/products` (list) and 40% `/products/<id>`. The `all_products` rebuild query takes ~200–400ms normally and has been observed in the 1–2s range while a bulk import is running (imports are batched upserts in transactions; that contention is exactly the observed latency-spike problem). During an import, 8 workers each re-running the rebuild at TTL expiry is plausibly 8 concurrent 1–2s queries — not fatal to Postgres, but it lands on the box at its worst moment.

3. **TTL implementation and deletions (Adversary Q3).** Plan: `cachetools.TTLCache` (per-entry expiry checked on access) rather than a hand-rolled dict with timestamps or a sweeper thread. Deleted products: no explicit invalidation, so a deleted product's `<id>` entry serves for up to 10 minutes after deletion, and it drops out of `all_products` when that key expires. Misses for nonexistent ids are not cached (no negative caching), so a scraper enumerating ids goes straight to Postgres every time.

4. **Scope of the PM's staleness sign-off (Advocate Q2, Judge Q2).** The PM approved "a product may be up to 10 minutes stale," full stop. The composite effects were not discussed: same-user consecutive requests disagreeing across workers, list vs. detail disagreeing, and mid-import mixed vintages were never put in front of the PM. Treat the sign-off as covering absolute age only.

5. **Downstream consumers (Advocate Q3).** Working assumption based on the doc's framing (these are catalog *read* endpoints, CDN was even considered): they serve browse/display traffic. Cart and checkout price from their own server-side path against Postgres and do not read through these endpoints. If that assumption is wrong anywhere, staleness stops being cosmetic — flagging it as a must-verify before build.

## Phase 3 — Adversary's Case

**Objection 1 — TTL expiry recreates the exact spike the design exists to prevent, and reintroduces the latency it exists to remove.**

- **Problem.** With lazy population and hard TTL expiry, the moment an `all_products` entry expires during a bulk import, every request that misses blocks on a rebuild query the Defender concedes runs 1–2s under import contention. Two compounding effects: (a) across 8 workers, up to 8 concurrent rebuilds land on Postgres at its worst moment; (b) within a worker, every list request arriving during the 1–2s rebuild window either dogpiles (more concurrent rebuilds if the worker handles requests concurrently) or queues behind it. At ~120–240 RPS on the list endpoint (60% of 200–400 RPS), a 1–2s rebuild means hundreds of requests per expiry event experience worse-than-baseline latency — during imports, which is the stated problem. The design's p95 during imports is governed by its worst 1–2s windows, not its sub-microsecond hits.
- **Evidence.** Defender's answer 2: rebuild observed at 1–2s during imports; imports are the observed latency-spike cause; "8 concurrent 1–2s queries... lands on the box at its worst moment" is the Defender's own words. Cache-stampede-at-expiry is a textbook failure mode of hard-TTL lazy caches; every mature cache design (stale-while-revalidate, request coalescing, probabilistic early expiration) exists because of it.
- **Better way.** Decouple refresh from the request path: soft TTL with serve-stale-while-revalidate — on expiry, one refresher (per-worker single-flight lock, or a background thread on a timer) rebuilds while all requests keep serving the stale entry. Staleness stays inside the 10-minute budget; no request ever blocks on the rebuild after first warmup; Postgres sees at most 8 sequentialized rebuilds per interval instead of a synchronized dogpile. This is ~30 lines, not new infra.

**Objection 2 — `serialized_product` is unpinned, and one wrong implementation choice silently serves region A's prices to region B.**

- **Problem.** The doc's cache shape (`{product_id: serialized_product}`) does not say whether the cached value is the region-neutral record or the rendered, region-priced response. If an implementer picks the latter (the natural reading of "serialized"), the cache key has no region dimension: whichever region populates the entry first defines the price every other region sees for up to 10 minutes. That is a pricing-correctness bug that no error is thrown for and no test currently guards.
- **Evidence.** Defender's answer 1 concedes exactly this: "the doc's phrase `serialized_product` does not pin this down, and if an implementer cached the rendered response instead, the cache would silently serve region A's prices to region B." The design itself proves regions get different prices — it rejects the CDN for precisely that reason.
- **Better way.** Amend the design to state explicitly: cached values are region-neutral Postgres records; pricing is applied post-cache-read at response assembly. Add one guard test: populate the cache via a region-A request, read via a region-B request, assert region-B pricing. Cheap, and it converts a silent correctness landmine into a spec'd invariant.

**Objection 3 — The PM approved "up to 10 minutes stale"; the design actually ships three anomalies the PM never saw.**

- **Problem.** Eight independent caches with two independent TTL clocks (`all_products` vs per-id) produce: (a) the same user getting new-then-old data on consecutive requests as the load balancer rotates workers; (b) the list view contradicting the detail view for the same product; (c) mid-import mixed vintages across products. None of these is "a product may be up to 10 minutes old" — they are visible inconsistencies, the kind that generate support tickets and post-launch rollback pressure. The staleness budget being spent is not the one that was approved.
- **Evidence.** Defender's answer 4: "The composite effects were not discussed... Treat the sign-off as covering absolute age only." The Defender listed all three anomalies unprompted in Phase 1 as the decisions they are least sure of.
- **Better way.** Two parts. (1) Put the three anomalies in front of the PM before build — one paragraph, five minutes. (2) Shrink the anomaly surface structurally: cache one atomically-swapped catalog snapshot per worker (a single object holding both the id-index and the assembled list), refreshed as a unit. That eliminates list-vs-detail disagreement entirely (one vintage per worker), collapses two TTL clocks into one, and composes with the Objection 1 background refresh. Cross-worker flip-flop remains, but it is now the only anomaly left to get approved.

**Objection 4 — Deletions and nonexistent ids are unhandled: takedowns linger, and enumeration traffic bypasses the cache entirely.**

- **Problem.** Two edges from Defender's answer 3. (a) A deleted product's detail entry serves for up to 10 minutes with no override path — acceptable for routine delistings, not for a mispriced-product or compliance takedown, where "we cannot remove it faster than 10 minutes without a deploy" is the wrong answer to give. (b) No negative caching: every request for a nonexistent id goes straight to Postgres. A scraper enumerating ids, or dangling links after an import removes products, produces uncached DB reads during exactly the import window the cache is meant to shield.
- **Evidence.** Defender's answer 3 states both behaviors explicitly: deleted entries serve until expiry; "misses for nonexistent ids are not cached... a scraper enumerating ids goes straight to Postgres every time."
- **Better way.** (a) Add a cheap kill switch: a cache-epoch value (env var or single tiny DB/config read on a coarse interval) that, when bumped, invalidates all entries — turns "10 minutes minimum" into "operator-controlled minutes" for emergencies without building an invalidation bus. (b) Cache negative results with the same TTL (a sentinel in the same structure, near-zero cost, bounded because the snapshot approach naturally answers "not in snapshot" for free).

**Objection 5 — The rollout proves nothing: staging lacks the load and the imports, and no success criteria or metrics are defined.**

- **Problem.** "Staging for a week, then production" validates only that the code doesn't crash. Staging has neither 200–400 RPS nor real bulk imports, so the two risks this review surfaced (expiry behavior under import load, composite staleness) are untested until full production exposure. There is also no defined metric to compare against the ~180ms baseline and no cache observability, so "did it work" and "is it misbehaving" are both unanswerable at flag-flip time.
- **Evidence.** Rollout section of the design is two lines: flag, staging week, production. No metrics, no exit criteria, no canary. Defender's answer 2 confirms the interesting behavior only occurs under production import contention.
- **Better way.** Define exit criteria before build: p95 target for the two endpoints, expected hit rate (>99% given 12k keys and this traffic), and a hard requirement that one production bulk import runs with the flag on during canary. Instrument hits/misses/refresh-duration/entry-age as metrics. Canary by worker subset or host subset in production rather than relying on a quiet staging week; the flag already makes this cheap.

## Phase 4 — Advocate's Case

### Part 1 — Steelman: why this design is right as-is

**1. The workload is close to the theoretical best case for caching, and the design matches it.** ~12k products, ~25MB serialized, ~50 writes/day against roughly 20–35M reads/day at the assumed 200–400 RPS — a read:write ratio on the order of 500,000:1, with an explicit, product-approved staleness budget. When the entire dataset fits in one worker's RAM many times over and writes are this rare, the textbook answer is the simplest possible cache, and that is what the design proposes. Elaborate coherence machinery here would be solving a problem the workload does not have.

**2. In-process is the only option that fully serves the stated goal.** The goal is latency *and* insulation from Postgres during imports. A dict hit removes both the DB round trip and any network hop; Redis removes only the first and adds a new one. Redis also adds provisioning, auth/TLS, monitoring, failover, connection-pool tuning, and a new production failure mode (Redis down or slow) for a dataset that fits in RAM eight times over. The in-process cache adds zero infrastructure and zero new failure modes: if a worker dies, its cache dies with it, and the degraded mode is exactly today's status quo. The on-call surface is unchanged.

**3. TTL-only invalidation buys off an entire bug class with an approved budget.** No invalidation bus, no pub/sub, no coherence protocol — the classically hard half of caching is traded for a staleness bound the PM signed. With `cachetools.TTLCache` the whole thing is on the order of 50 lines behind a flag. Cheap to build, cheap to revert, cheap to reason about.

**4. Most of the Adversary's case is not about the in-process choice at all.** The user's question is "is the in-process cache going to bite us?" Objections 1, 4, and most of 3 apply identically to a Redis cache with per-key TTLs: Redis stampedes at expiry, Redis serves deleted products until expiry, Redis holds mixed vintages across keys. These are properties of TTL caching as such, and every one has a small in-process remedy. Only the cross-worker flip-flop (part of Objection 3) is specific to per-worker caches — and it is bounded by the TTL and rare in practice (see answer 3). Nothing in the Adversary's case argues for a different architecture; every "better way" is an amendment *inside* this design. That is the strongest possible endorsement of the skeleton.

### Part 2 — Answers to the five objections

**Objection 1 (expiry stampede) — dispute the problem's magnitude; accept the mitigation as a small hardening, not a redesign.**
The claim that "p95 during imports is governed by its worst 1–2s windows" fails arithmetic on the Adversary's own numbers. Per worker: ~15–30 RPS of list traffic (60% of 200–400 RPS across 8 workers), one `all_products` expiry per 10 minutes, a 1–2s rebuild. That is ~30–60 affected requests out of ~9,000–18,000 per worker per interval — roughly 0.3%. That is a p99+ event, not a p95 event; p95 is governed by the sub-millisecond hits. Note also that lazy population *staggers* expiry across workers (each worker's clock starts at its own first miss), so "8 simultaneous rebuilds" is the pathological alignment, not the steady state; and with sync gunicorn workers there is no intra-worker dogpile at all — one request per worker at a time. Compare the baseline: today *every* list request pays the full query, including the 1–2s import-window cost. The cache converts "everyone pays" into "~0.3% pay." That said, the serve-stale/single-flight refresh is ~30 lines, composes with everything else, and removes even the p99 blip — I accept it as an amendment. What I dispute is the framing: this is a hardening of a sound design, not evidence the design recreates the problem it solves.

**Objection 2 (region-pricing ambiguity) — concede the doc defect; dispute that it is a design defect.**
The Defender conceded the wording and I will not pretend otherwise: `serialized_product` must be pinned down. But the design's own logic already answers the question — it rejects the CDN *because* pricing is per-region and applied at the app layer, which entails that the cacheable object is the region-neutral record and pricing happens post-read. The intended architecture is correct and internally consistent; the failure mode requires an implementer to contradict the design's stated rationale. The fix is one sentence in the doc plus the Adversary's guard test (populate as region A, read as region B, assert region-B pricing), both of which I accept. This objection amends a paragraph; it does not touch a decision.

**Objection 3 (composite staleness anomalies) — accept the PM conversation; dispute the severity; dispute half the better way.**
Accepting outright: putting the three anomalies in front of the PM is five minutes and should happen. On severity, I dispute the "support tickets and rollback pressure" prediction. These endpoints serve browse/display traffic; checkout prices from its own Postgres path (flagged as a must-verify, and I join that flag). At ~50 writes/day over 12k products, a given product changes about four times a day at most under any plausible skew; each change opens a ≤10-minute disagreement window; a user must view that same product twice inside that window *and* land on workers holding different vintages to notice anything. This is a rare, cosmetic event on a browse surface — the same class of event any multi-node TTL cache (including Redis read paths and today's mid-import uncached reads across a multi-transaction import) already produces. On the better way, I split it: the PM paragraph, yes; the atomic-snapshot restructure, not yet. It abandons lazy per-key population (first detail request pays the full 12k-row rebuild), collapses a design the PM may accept as-is, and is precisely the speculative restructuring we should not fund before the five-minute conversation returns an objection. If the PM balks, the snapshot is the right shape and I will support it then.

**Objection 4 (deletions and negative caching) — dispute the problem on both halves.**
(a) The takedown scenario's premise — "we cannot remove it faster than 10 minutes without a deploy" — is false for an in-process cache. The cache lives in worker memory: a rolling worker restart (or flipping the already-designed `CATALOG_CACHE` flag) clears every entry with zero deploy and zero new code. The design already contains its emergency kill switch; the proposed cache-epoch mechanism rebuilds, more elaborately, a capability the process model gives us for free. It also guards a scenario (compliance takedown with sub-10-minute SLA) that appears nowhere in the requirements. (b) On negative caching: a miss for a nonexistent id is a primary-key point lookup returning zero rows — the cheapest query Postgres executes — and its rate under the cache is *identical to today*, when every request of every kind hits Postgres. The cache makes nothing worse; scraper enumeration is a rate-limiting concern that predates and outlives this design. A negative-sentinel entry is near-free and I do not oppose it as optional hardening, but it is not fixing a defect.

**Objection 5 (rollout proves nothing) — accept the evidence; dispute the conclusion; accept the amendment.**
"No metrics, no exit criteria" is factually right and I concede the rollout section is underspecified — instrumenting hits/misses/refresh-duration/entry-age and defining a p95 target and hit-rate expectation before build is standard practice and costs little. But "proves nothing" overstates. The staging week validates exactly the things staging can validate: correctness of the pricing-after-cache-read path, TTL mechanics, deletion behavior, serialization — where the silent bugs from Objection 2 would surface. And the production-import canary the Adversary demands is *enabled by the design's own flag*: canary by worker or host subset during a real import is a two-line ops procedure precisely because the design was built flag-first. The better way here is a checklist the existing design already supports, not a correction of it.

**Summary.** Two objections amend documentation and rollout hygiene (2, 5 — accepted as amendments), one is a cheap hardening of a sound mechanism (1 — accepted as ~30 lines, framing disputed), one is a five-minute stakeholder confirmation plus a premature restructure (3 — split), and one rests on a false premise about the process model (4 — disputed). None argues against the architecture. The design as written, plus one sentence, one test, one refresh guard, and one metrics paragraph, is the best plan on the table.

## Phase 5 — Adversary's Rebuttal

**Objection 1 (expiry stampede): DROPPED.** The Advocate's arithmetic convinced me on the headline claim: at 15-30 list RPS per worker, one expiry per 10-minute interval, and a 1-2s rebuild, roughly 0.2-0.3% of a worker's requests land in a rebuild window. One note for the record so the accepted amendment is not later downgraded to "optional": the "lazy population staggers expiry" defense is fragile, because any deploy or rolling restart cold-starts all workers within one window and aligns their TTL clocks from then on (gunicorn `max_requests` worker recycling re-scrambles and re-aligns them at arbitrary times). The amendment both sides now endorse is precisely what makes that alignment harmless, which is why it belongs in v1, where the Advocate has already put it.

**Objection 2 (region-pricing ambiguity): DROPPED.** The Advocate accepted both remedies in full: the design will state that cached values are region-neutral records priced post-read, and the cross-region guard test will exist. Whether we file it as a doc defect or a design defect changes nothing once the sentence and the test are in; a spec'd invariant with a failing-test tripwire is exactly what I asked for.

**Objection 3 (composite staleness anomalies): SUSTAINED, on both disputed halves.**

They are not. The write workload is bulk imports, batched upserts in transactions per the Defender's answer 2, which is correlated change by definition. Immediately after an import commits, every worker holds some mix of pre- and post-import vintages across `all_products` and the per-id entries, for every product the import touched, for up to 10 minutes. The canonical browse flow (list, then click through to detail) crosses the two independent TTL clocks exactly where they disagree, so post-import the list-vs-detail inconsistency is elevated for the import's entire product set at once, not for one product at a time. *The snapshot's cost.* The Advocate rejects the atomic snapshot because "the first detail request pays the full 12k-row rebuild" and calls it speculative restructuring. The snapshot is the same query plus a dict comprehension to index it by id; the per-id lazy path it "abandons" is replaced by a lookup into rows the worker already fetched. And once the Objection 1 amendment the Advocate accepted is in place, the rebuild runs in the background and no request pays it at all after warmup. Net effect: one structure, one TTL clock, one refresh path, fewer lines than two coordinated caches, and list-vs-detail disagreement becomes structurally impossible instead of PM-tolerated. It also delivers, as free side effects, the two behaviors I conceded in Objection 4: deletions drop out within one refresh cycle, and "not in snapshot" answers nonexistent-id probes without touching Postgres. I accept the Advocate's sequencing on the stakeholder half (the PM paragraph first; it is five minutes) but reject gating the snapshot on the PM balking: with the background refresh already accepted, the snapshot is the simpler shape of this design, not an elaboration to fund reluctantly. The only anomaly left for the PM to approve is the cross-worker flip-flop, which no in-process design removes.

**Objection 4 (deletions and negative caching): DROPPED.** Convinced on both halves. (a) The process model already contains the kill switch: a rolling worker restart or a `CATALOG_CACHE` flag flip empties every cache with zero new code, and the post-flush load is just today's baseline. My cache-epoch proposal rebuilt, more elaborately, a capability the design gets for free, guarding a sub-10-minute takedown SLA that appears nowhere in the requirements. (b) A nonexistent-id miss is a primary-key point lookup whose rate under the cache is identical to the uncached status quo; the cache makes nothing worse, and scraper enumeration is a rate-limiting problem this design neither causes nor should solve. (The snapshot happens to subsume both behaviors, but that is an argument inside Objection 3, not a resurrection of this one.)

**Objection 5 (rollout proves nothing): DROPPED.** The Advocate accepted the substance: instrument hits/misses/refresh duration/entry age, define the p95 target and expected hit rate before build, and run a production canary during a real bulk import using the existing flag. "Proves nothing" was my overstatement; staging does validate the correctness paths, including the Objection 2 guard test. The one procedural hold I keep is already conceded by the Advocate's own "underspecified" admission: the checklist must be written into the rollout section of the design doc before build, not carried as debate-transcript folklore.

**Net position.** One objection sustained (3), four dropped (1, 2, 4, 5), three of those dropped because the Advocate accepted the substantive remedy, not because the problem was imaginary. The surviving disagreement is narrow and concrete: I say the atomic snapshot ships now, because it is net-simpler once the accepted background refresh exists and the import-correlated anomaly window is a real daily event; the Advocate says only if the PM objects. ## Phase 6 — Advocate's Surrebuttal

**1. Objection 3 (composite staleness anomalies / atomic snapshot): CONCEDED.** I withdraw my "not yet" on the snapshot and support shipping it in v1. Two proofs convinced me, and I want them on the record precisely.

*First, the cost objection was my arithmetic error.* My Phase 4 case against the snapshot rested on "the first detail request pays the full 12k-row rebuild" — but the design as written already runs that exact query to rebuild `all_products` every <=10 minutes per worker. The snapshot's marginal cost over the design I was defending is a dict comprehension over rows the worker has already fetched. I was pricing the full query as new cost when it was already in the budget; once the accounting is corrected, the snapshot is strictly cheaper than two coordinated caches: one structure, one TTL clock, one refresh path, and with the Objection 1 background refresh (which I accepted in Phase 4) no request pays the rebuild after warmup. A cost objection that survives only under wrong accounting is not a cost objection.

*Second, the severity counterexample broke my probability model.* My "rare cosmetic coincidence" math assumed writes are independent and uniformly spread. The Defender's answer 2 says the write workload is bulk imports — batched upserts in transactions — which is correlated change by definition. After an import commits, the anomaly window opens for the import's entire product set simultaneously, across every worker, and the canonical browse flow (list, then click to detail) crosses the two TTL clocks exactly where they disagree. If imports run daily, that is a scheduled daily anomaly event, not the per-product coincidence I priced. My severity estimate was an average over a distribution the workload does not have.

I also note the concession is reinforced, not just permitted, by the Adversary's Objection 4 observation: the snapshot delivers deletion-drop-out within one refresh cycle and free "not in snapshot" answers for nonexistent ids — two behaviors we otherwise left unhandled — as side effects of the simpler structure.
