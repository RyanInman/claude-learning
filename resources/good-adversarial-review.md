# What Makes a Good Adversarial Review of AI Chatbots

## TL;DR
- A high-quality adversarial review (red team / safety evaluation) of an AI chatbot is defined less by clever jailbreaks than by **discipline**: an explicitly scoped threat model tied to real-world impact, a diverse and partly independent team, a reproducible and coverage-driven methodology combining human probing with automation, severity-graded findings with reproducible transcripts, and a closed remediation-and-retest loop that runs continuously rather than once.
- The strongest published guidance converges on the same pillars: NIST (AI RMF, the Generative AI Profile AI 600-1, and the adversarial-ML taxonomy AI 100-2e2025), OWASP's GenAI Red Teaming Guide and LLM Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, and lab practices from Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind. All stress testing the **end-to-end system** (model + guardrails + RAG + tools + integrations), not just the model in isolation.
- Weak reviews are "security theater": vague purpose, undefined success criteria, English-only and homogeneous testers, one-off point-in-time snapshots, no severity grading or root-cause analysis, no retest after fixes, and reliance on cleverness or benchmark scores instead of realistic, impact-anchored coverage.

## Key Findings

**1. Scope and threat model must be explicit and impact-first.** The single most common failure identified in the literature is starting from attack techniques rather than from the harms that matter. Microsoft's rule ("Understand what the system can do and where it is applied") and its practice of reasoning backward from downstream Impact are the benchmark; NIST AI 600-1 asks reviewers to fix the lifecycle phase, the scope of risk (model / infrastructure / ecosystem), and the source of risk before testing.

**2. Tester diversity and partial independence materially improve coverage.** NIST AI 600-1 states that "demographically and interdisciplinarily diverse AI red teams" find flaws in the varying contexts where GenAI is used, and that red teams should have domain expertise and socio-cultural awareness of the deployment context. OpenAI selects cohorts across professional background, education, gender, age, geography, and languages spoken. Independence is a spectrum from first-party product teams through dedicated internal red teams to fully independent third parties; each adds coverage and reduces "marking your own homework."

**3. Methodology must be systematic, taxonomy-anchored, and reproducible.** Good reviews map findings to shared taxonomies (OWASP LLM Top 10 2025, MITRE ATLAS, NIST AI 100-2e2025) and produce reproducible artifacts—prompt/response pairs, full transcripts, procedures, and root-cause (weakness) analysis. Coverage should span the recognized attack categories: prompt injection (direct and indirect), jailbreaks (narrow and universal), sensitive-data/system-prompt extraction, harmful-content elicitation, bias/toxicity, hallucination/misinformation, and multi-turn manipulation.

**4. Combine expert manual probing with automation.** Human creativity finds novel and socio-technical harms; automation (e.g., Microsoft's PyRIT, automated multi-turn attacks like Crescendo, OpenAI's auto-generated-reward RL approach) scales coverage and frees humans for high-value work. Neither alone is sufficient.

**5. Realistic scenarios and system-level coverage.** The best reviews emulate real adversaries and benign users against the deployed end-to-end system—guardrails, RAG/vector stores, tool calls, and integrations. As Microsoft's team concluded, "relatively simple attacks that target weaknesses in end-to-end systems are more likely to be successful than complex algorithms that target only the underlying AI model."

**6. Severity grading, clear success criteria, and closed remediation loops.** Findings need defined success criteria and a severity model; retesting after fixes and continuous testing across the lifecycle are essential. Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers bug-bounty program is a model of strict success criteria and retest-after-fix.

## Details

### Scope, threat model, and success criteria
Feffer et al. (CMU/SEI, "Red-Teaming for Generative AI: Silver Bullet or Security Theater?", AAAI/ACM AIES 2024) found that AI red-teaming practices "diverge along several axes, including the purpose of the activity (which is often vague)," and argued that before red-teaming one should define: the artifact under evaluation, the threat model, the specific vulnerability, the criteria for assessing success, team composition, resources, instructions, model access, and permitted methods—plus what documentation will be produced. Their conclusion: gestures toward red-teaming as a "panacea" without this structure "verge on security theater."

NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1, July 2024) grounds scope in risk: Section 2 urges structuring red teaming around lifecycle phase (design, dev, deployment, operation, decommission), the scope of the risk (model, infrastructure, or ecosystem), and the source of the risk. The profile defines 12 GenAI risk categories (including CBRN information/uplift, confabulation/hallucination, data privacy, harmful bias, obscene/abusive content, information security including prompt injection and poisoning, and value-chain/component integration), and organizes suggested actions under Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Crucially, red teaming appears under Measure as a recurring—not one-time—activity.

Microsoft's "Lessons from Red Teaming 100 Generative AI Products" (Bullwinkel et al., January 2025) makes scope its first lesson ("Understand what the system can do and where it is applied") and recommends starting from downstream Impact and working backward to attack paths, so findings tie to real-world risk. Its ontology models System, Actor (adversarial or benign), TTPs (mapped to MITRE ATT&CK and ATLAS), Weakness, and Impact.

### Diversity, expertise, and independence
OpenAI's white paper "OpenAI's Approach to External Red Teaming for AI Models and Systems" (Lama Ahmad, Sandhini Agarwal, Michael Lampe, Pamela Mishkin, 2025; arXiv 2503.16431) lays out four design steps for a campaign: (1) "Deciding the composition of the red teaming cohort based on the outlined goals and prioritized domains for testing"; (2) "Determining the versions of the model or system to which the red teamers will have access"; (3) "Creating and providing interfaces, instructions, and documentation guidance to red teamers"; and (4) "Synthesizing the data and creating evaluations." It states the ideal cohort "may vary across axes such as professional background, education level, gender, age, geographic location, and languages spoken," and that external red teamers "bring valuable context, such as knowledge of regional politics, cultural contexts, or technical fields like law and medicine." OpenAI names four goals: discovery of novel risks, stress-testing mitigations, augmenting risk assessment with domain expertise, and independent assessment. It has conducted external red teaming since DALL·E 2 (2022), with documented efforts in the System Cards for GPT-4, GPT-4(V), DALL·E 3, GPT-4o, and o1.

Anthropic's approach spans four methods—domain-specific expert red teaming, using language models to red team, red teaming in new modalities, and open-ended general red teaming—and its Trust & Safety "Policy Vulnerability Testing" partners with external subject-matter experts (e.g., Thorn on child safety, the Institute for Strategic Dialogue on election integrity, the Global Project Against Hate and Extremism on radicalization).

Independence is increasingly treated as a first-class requirement. Stanford HAI and collaborators ("In-House Evaluation Is Not Enough," 2025) place evaluations on a spectrum from first-party (limited, e.g., product teams), first-party (expansive, e.g., Microsoft's dedicated AI Red Team), second-party (contracted), to third-party (no contractual relationship), arguing developers should not "mark their own homework" and calling for coordinated flaw disclosure and legal safe harbors for good-faith researchers. The UK AI Safety/Security Institute (AISI) built one of the largest public evaluation teams, published the open-source Inspect framework, and runs pre-deployment tests—though critics note reliance on developer cooperation limits full independence.

### Systematic, reproducible methodology and coverage
OWASP's GenAI Red Teaming Guide (Jan 2025) recommends a lifecycle approach across four phases—model evaluation, implementation testing, system/infrastructure evaluation, and runtime analysis—viewed through security (operator), safety (users), and trust (users). The companion OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) provides the vulnerability taxonomy, adding System Prompt Leakage and Vector & Embedding Weaknesses (RAG risks), renaming Overreliance to Misinformation, broadening Model DoS to Unbounded Consumption, and expanding Excessive Agency. An OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026) extends this to tool-using and multi-agent systems.

MITRE ATLAS supplies an ATT&CK-style knowledge base of adversary tactics, techniques, and real-world case studies against AI systems; recent releases added generative-AI techniques including RAG poisoning, false RAG entry injection, LLM prompt crafting, impersonation, and AI supply-chain compromise. NIST AI 100-2e2025 (Vassilev, Oprea, et al., March 2025) is the authoritative adversarial-ML taxonomy, covering evasion, poisoning, and privacy attacks for predictive AI and extending to supply-chain attacks, direct/indirect prompt injection, misuse, and agent security for generative AI, each paired with mitigations. Reproducibility means recording prompt/generation pairs or full conversations, category/domain, and severity on a specified scale, plus procedures to reproduce and weakness/root-cause analysis.

### Manual + automated testing
Microsoft's Lesson 4/5 pairing—"Automation can help cover more of the risk landscape" and "The human element of AI red teaming is crucial"—captures the consensus. Microsoft open-sourced PyRIT for scaled testing. OpenAI's second 2024 paper introduced auto-generated rewards and multi-step reinforcement learning to diversify automated attacks. Automated multi-turn attacks matter: Microsoft's Crescendo (Russinovich et al.) gradually escalates from benign prompts, and its automation Crescendomation (now in PyRIT) "surpasses other state-of-the-art jailbreaking techniques on the AdvBench subset dataset, achieving 29–61% higher performance on GPT-4 and 49–71% on Gemini-Pro" (arXiv:2404.01833, USENIX Security 2025), with binary success of 98% on GPT-4. Multi-turn manipulation is a distinct, high-value category because single-turn defenses often fail to catch gradual escalation.

### Severity grading and success criteria
CVSS remains useful for technical AI-security findings but is widely viewed as insufficient for safety findings where impact is user-harm, brand, or regulatory. Practitioners (e.g., Intigriti) argue AI-safety severity should be outcome-based—defined by harm category, affected audience, real-world exposure, and worst-case scenario—rather than by the cleverness of a jailbreak. Anthropic proposed a jailbreak scoring framework (capability gain, etc.) explicitly modeled on CVSS. Attack Success Rate (ASR) is the most common metric but is coarse; newer work proposes finer-grained and risk-adjusted harm scoring, and one 2026 methodological paper warns that ASR comparisons across studies often lack valid measurement.

### Closed remediation loop and continuous testing
Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers work is a strong exemplar of success criteria + retest. A HackerOne bug-bounty program invited experienced red teamers (405 participants invited from ~800 applicants; a February 2025 public challenge drew 339 participants and over 300,000 interactions across 8 CBRN difficulty levels) with a strict definition of "universal jailbreak" (must extract harmful responses across all forbidden queries). On 10,000 synthetic prompts against Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the first-generation classifiers cut the jailbreak success rate from a baseline of 86% to 4.4% ("over 95% of jailbreak attempts were refused"), albeit at a 23.7% compute-cost increase and a 0.38% rise in refusals. The follow-up "Constitutional Classifiers++" (released January 9, 2026) achieved similar robustness "at just ~1% additional compute cost," with no universal jailbreak found after further red-teaming—illustrating iterate-test-fix-retest. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework ties periodic "early warning" evaluations to Critical Capability Levels across CBRN, cybersecurity, ML R&D, and (in the third iteration) harmful manipulation, with internal and external expertise and governance sign-off. Microsoft's Lesson 8—"The work of securing AI systems will never be complete"—makes continuity explicit.

### Common pitfalls of weak reviews
- **Vague or missing purpose and success criteria** (Feffer et al.): no defined artifact, threat model, or pass/fail bar.
- **Model-only focus**: neglecting production system, guardrails, RAG, and tool/agent integrations where real impact occurs (Bullwinkel et al.).
- **Homogeneous, English-only testing**: most processes remain limited to English and lack linguistic/socio-cultural expertise; insider risks are often ignored.
- **One-off snapshots**: point-in-time findings become stale as models change (OpenAI limitation: "Relevance across evolving models and systems").
- **Benchmark confusion**: treating red teaming as benchmarking (Microsoft Lesson 3)—benchmarks measure known harms on curated data; red teaming explores unknowns.
- **No standardized reporting or disclosure**: opacity prevents validation and mitigation tracking; unclear whether findings were ever fixed.
- **Rewarding cleverness over impact**: elegant bypasses that produce low-value output over blunt but genuinely harmful outcomes.
- **Ignoring reviewer welfare**: exposure to harmful content causes psychological harm; fair compensation, informed consent, and mental-health support are part of a credible program (OpenAI, and sociotechnical literature).

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Before testing (scope & design).** Write a one-page charter defining: the artifact and its deployment context; the threat model(s) and in-scope adversaries/benign-failure modes; prioritized harm categories mapped to NIST AI 600-1's 12 categories and the OWASP LLM Top 10; explicit success criteria and a severity rubric (use CVSS for security findings, an outcome-based scale for safety findings); team composition (domain experts, multiple languages, demographic diversity, and at least some independence from builders); and model-access tier. *Change trigger:* if you cannot state the threat model and success criteria, do not proceed—you are heading toward security theater.

**Stage 2 — Execution (coverage).** Run both manual expert probing and automated/scaled testing (e.g., PyRIT-style tooling, automated multi-turn attacks). Cover, at minimum: direct and indirect prompt injection, narrow and universal jailbreaks, system-prompt/data extraction, harmful-content elicitation, bias/toxicity, hallucination/misinformation, and multi-turn manipulation—each tested at model, guardrail, RAG, and tool/agent levels. Map every technique to MITRE ATLAS / NIST AI 100-2e2025. *Change trigger:* add agentic/multi-agent tests (OWASP ASI Top 10) whenever the chatbot gains tool use, memory, or autonomy.

**Stage 3 — Reporting.** For each finding, deliver a reproducible transcript, the mapped taxonomy category, a severity score with justification, root-cause (weakness) analysis, and a suggested mitigation owner. Distinguish safety vs. security impact. Handle information hazards with access controls and responsible disclosure.

**Stage 4 — Remediate, retest, repeat.** Feed findings into fixes, then re-run the exact failing cases plus generalizations to confirm the fix and check for regressions (the Constitutional Classifiers loop). Convert high-value manual findings into automated regression evaluations. Institutionalize continuous testing across the lifecycle, re-testing after every material model, prompt, guardrail, or integration change. Engage independent third parties and/or a bug-bounty program for high-stakes deployments. *Benchmarks that should change your posture:* a rising ASR on your regression suite, discovery of any universal jailbreak, or a new capability/modality/integration should each trigger a fresh scoped campaign.

## Caveats
- **Field immaturity and contested terms.** There is no industry consensus on the scope, structure, or assessment criteria for AI red teaming; "red teaming" is used loosely for everything from security pentests to open-ended safety probing. Treat any single framework as necessary but not sufficient.
- **Red teaming is not a panacea.** Even its practitioners (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Feffer et al.) describe it as a qualitative precursor to quantitative evaluation, not a safety guarantee; it can miss important risk classes and cannot certify a system "safe."
- **Metric fragility.** ASR is coarse and often not comparable across studies; CVSS under-captures safety harms. Severity and success metrics remain active research areas.
- **Independence is imperfect in practice.** Most public frontier evaluations are post-mitigation and depend on developer cooperation and developer-selected evaluators; genuinely independent, pre-mitigation evaluation with legal safe harbors is still emerging.
- **Vendor and secondary sources.** Some figures here come from vendor blogs and secondary write-ups; where possible they are corroborated against primary papers (NIST, arXiv, OWASP, lab papers). Rapidly evolving 2026 items (e.g., NIST CAISI agent initiatives, ATLAS version counts) should be reverified before operational use.
- **Forward-looking framing.** Several cited practices (e.g., DeepMind's harmful-manipulation CCL, agentic red-teaming standards) describe evolving or partially implemented programs rather than settled, proven methods.
