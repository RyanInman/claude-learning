    1| # Reviewing AI-Generated Output: Principles and Techniques for Comprehension Under Load
    2| 
    3| ## TL;DR
    4| - The single highest-leverage move is to **stop reviewing AI code as a raw diff and instead review re-presentations of it** — layered summaries, the AI's own stated intent and assumptions, tests as behavior specs, and natural-language back-translations — because comprehension, not typing, is now the bottleneck, and reading code is cognitively harder than writing it.
    5| - The cognitive science is unambiguous: working memory holds only about four chunks, defect detection collapses past ~400 lines of code (LOC) per review and after ~60–90 minutes, and AI output uniquely triggers automation bias (in Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh's ACM CCS 2023 study of 47 participants, those with an AI assistant "wrote significantly less secure code" yet "were more likely to believe they wrote secure code"), so re-presentation techniques that cut extraneous load and force active engagement pay off more per unit of effort than working harder on the raw code.
    6| - Prioritized by comprehension-per-effort: (1) review the plan/spec before code; (2) demand AI self-explanation with an explicit "least-confident" section; (3) read tests as executable specifications; (4) chunk and tier review by risk; (5) use multi-level summaries and diagrams; (6) use an adversarial second model for back-translation. Keep a named human accountable for every merge.
    7| 
    8| ## Key Findings
    9| 
   10| 1. **Comprehension is now the constraint, and it is expensive.** Developers spend a majority of their time reading rather than writing code, and reading is harder because the reader must reconstruct context and intent the author held implicitly. AI shifts even more volume onto the reading side, so the bottleneck migrated from generation into the review queue.
   11| 
   12| 2. **Working memory is tiny; reviews must be sized to it.** Modern cognitive-load theory puts usable working memory at roughly four chunks. The SmartBear/Cisco case study (the Cisco Unified MeetingPlace team over roughly 10 months, described as "the world's largest published case study of peer code review, spanning 2500 reviews of 3.2 million lines of code") found defect detection is highest under 200 LOC, falls off past 400 LOC, and that reviewers "faster than 450 lines/hour" had below-average defect density "in 87% of the cases"; 200–400 LOC over 60–90 minutes yields "70–90% defect discovery." These are reader limits measured on human-written code, so they bound AI review just as hard.
   13| 
   14| 3. **AI output triggers over-trust.** In the Stanford study "Do Users Write More Insecure Code with AI Assistants?" (Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh, ACM CCS 2023, 47 participants, OpenAI's codex-davinci-002), participants with an AI assistant "wrote significantly less secure code than those without access" and "were more likely to believe they wrote secure code," while those "who trusted the AI less and engaged more with... their prompts... provided code with fewer security vulnerabilities." Automation bias plus vigilance decrement (performance drops after the first half hour of monitoring) means reviewers of reliable-looking AI code drift toward rubber-stamping.
   15| 
   16| 4. **AI code fails in distinctive ways.** "Asleep at the Keyboard?" (Pearce, Ahmad, Tan, Dolan-Gavitt & Karri, IEEE S&P 2022) generated 1,689 programs from 89 security-relevant scenarios and "found approximately 40% to be vulnerable" (precisely 39.33% of top suggestions). GitClear's 2025 "AI Copilot Code Quality" report (211M changed lines, 2020–2024) shows moved/refactored code fell from 24.8% of changed lines in 2021 to 9.5% in 2024, copy/paste rose from 8.4% to 12.3% (exceeding moved code for the first time), and commits containing blocks of 5+ duplicated lines rose eightfold in 2024 — AI code is plausible-looking but redundancy-prone and codebase-unaware. Package hallucination ("slopsquatting") is a new supply-chain vector.
   17| 
   18| 5. **Re-presentation works because it converts extraneous load into germane load.** Multi-level summaries, diagrams (dual coding), and natural-language outlines let a reviewer form a top-down mental model first, then confirm it against beacons in the code — matching how expert program comprehension actually works.
   19| 
   20| 6. **Reviewers actually feel *better* about AI PRs even when they are worse.** The MSR 2026 study "More Code, Less Reuse" (Huang et al.) found the Average Max Redundancy for AI agents was 0.2867 versus 0.1532 for humans (a ~1.87x increase, p<0.001), yet "reviewers tend to express more neutral or positive emotions towards AI-generated contributions than human ones." The authors call this a "reviewer's blind spot": surface-level plausibility masks redundancy, "leading to the silent accumulation of technical debt." Countering this disconnect is the core job of the workflow.

   22| ## Details
   23| 
   24| ### 1. Cognitive foundations (why re-presentation helps)
   25| 
   26| **Cognitive load theory.** Sweller's framework divides load into intrinsic (inherent task complexity), extraneous (how information is presented), and germane (effort that builds durable schemas). Working memory is limited — classically 7±2, revised down to about four chunks — while long-term memory is effectively unlimited and stores knowledge as schemas. The design lever is direct: **reduce extraneous load so more capacity is free for intrinsic content and schema-building.** Every re-presentation technique in this report is an extraneous-load reduction.
   27| 
   28| **Chunking and schemas.** Experts read code by recognizing patterns ("this is the Observer pattern," "standard auth flow") as single chunks; novices process line by line. Re-presenting code grouped into named logical sections does the chunking for the reviewer, freeing working memory.
   29| 
   30| **Program comprehension: top-down vs bottom-up, beacons, plans.** Research (Brooks; Soloway & Ehrlich; Pennington; von Mayrhauser & Vans) shows two strategies. **Top-down** (used when the domain is familiar): the reader forms hypotheses about intent and confirms them via **beacons** — recognizable cues like method names and stereotypical code sequences. **Bottom-up** (unfamiliar code): the reader chunks individual lines into higher-level abstractions. Experts go top-down when they can. Implication: give the reviewer the intent *first* so they can review top-down, and make beacons salient.
   31| 
   32| **Dual coding.** Paivio's theory holds that information encoded in both verbal and visual channels creates two retrieval traces and distributes load across separate subsystems (the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad). Pairing a diagram (control flow, data flow, dependency graph) with concise text measurably improves comprehension and recall over text alone — the caveat being that decorative or redundant visuals *add* load, so visuals must explain, not decorate.
   33| 
   34| **Reading vs writing.** Writing is generative from a complete mental model; reading is reconstruction of a model the author never wrote down. Kernighan's dictum ("debugging is twice as hard as writing") generalizes: reviewing AI code is harder than the AI's writing of it, and the volume is now many times larger.
   35| 
   36| ### 2. Code-review empirical findings
   37| 
   38| - **Size:** Under 200 LOC per review is optimal; detection degrades past 400 LOC (SmartBear/Cisco). Reviews of 200–400 LOC over 60–90 minutes yield 70–90% defect discovery.
   39| - **Speed/duration:** Reviewers slower than ~400–500 LOC/hour are above average at finding defects; above 450 LOC/hour, defect density is below average in ~87% of cases. Detection collapses after 60–90 minutes.
   40| - **Author annotation:** In the Cisco data, authors who annotated their changes before review produced markedly lower defect densities — the mechanism being that annotating forces the author to self-review. This is the human precedent for making AI annotate its own output.
   41| - **Checklists vs ad-hoc:** Evidence is mixed but leans toward checklist-based reading being superior in effectiveness, efficiency, and false-positive rate; checklists are especially good at catching *omissions*, the hardest defect class ("hard to review something that isn't there").
   42| - **What review is really for (Bacchelli & Bird, "Expectations, Outcomes, and Challenges of Modern Code Review," ICSE 2013, Microsoft; 200 threads / 570 comments across teams):** "while finding defects remains the main motivation for review, reviews are less about defects than expected," with "code and change understanding" being "the key aspect of code reviewing." Defect comments were only about one-eighth of the sample. Modern review is fundamentally a comprehension exercise, which is why comprehension aids are the right intervention.
